# Research: the ideal system and its anatomy as a design benchmark

Flow 33752b, 2026-10-12. Web research, authorized. Question: who has held and
developed the idea of an ideal system whose enumerated parts serve as a
benchmark against which a design is asked "what are we missing?", and what
anatomy each gives.

Verification marks. **[source]**: quote read in the primary text (PDF or page
fetched this session). **[secondary]**: quote read only in a reproduction or
summary by someone else; the primary was not reached. **[not verified]**:
attribution found in searches only. Quotes are kept under 40 words.

## 1. Russell L. Ackoff — idealized design

Work: "Idealized Design: Creative Corporate Visioning", *Omega* 21(4),
401–410, 1993, https://isfcolombia.uniandes.edu.co/images/2020-intersemestral/23_de_junio/ackoff1993.pdf
[source]; Ackoff, Magidson, Addison, *Idealized Design*, Wharton School
Publishing 2006, sample chapter
https://ptgmedia.pearsoncmg.com/images/9780137071111/samplepages/0137071116.pdf [source].

Idea (2006, Introduction) [source]: "the way to get the best outcome is to
imagine what the ideal solution would be and then work backward to where you
are today. This ensures that you do not erect imaginary obstacles before you
even know what the ideal is."

Idea (1993, p. 401) [source]: "Once such a design is completed, planning can
be directed at closing the gaps between what the organization actually is and
the idealized design."

Anatomy. The ideal is not enumerated as parts; it is bounded by three
constraints the design must satisfy (1993, pp. 402–403) [source]:
(1) "technologically feasible"; (2) "operationally viable ... must be capable
of surviving if it did [come into existence]"; (3) "subject to continuous
improvement from within and without", i.e. "(a) capable of rapid and
effective learning and adaptation, and (b) subject to change by those who
hold a stake in it." Hence "the product of such a design is the best
ideal-seeking system that its designers can conceive of at the time." Two
versions: constrained (containing systems unchanged) and unconstrained.

Process (2006, ch. 1) [source]: Idealization — 1 Formulating the mess, 2 Ends
planning; Realization — 3 Means planning, 4 Resource planning, 5 Design of
implementation, 6 Design of controls.

Gap finding (2006, ch. 1, Ends planning) [source]: "It then identifies the gaps
between this idealized design and the organization as it is, thus revealing
the gaps to be filled by the rest of the planning process." The starting
move is the Bell Labs 1951 premise, "Gentlemen, the telephone system of the
United States was destroyed last night" [source], so the ideal is designed
from nothing and the present is measured against it.

## 2. Genrich Altshuller — Ideal Final Result, ideality, completeness of parts (TRIZ)

Works: *Creativity as an Exact Science* (Moscow 1979; English, Gordon &
Breach 1984); *And Suddenly the Inventor Appeared* (Technical Innovation
Center, 1996), which has a chapter titled "The Ideal Machine is No Machine"
(p. 106) [not verified: chapter title seen in a search summary only].

Idea [secondary]: MATRIZ wiki, "Ideal system",
https://wiki.matriz.org/.../ideal-system-5939/ : "It may have no components or
associated costs, but still deliver the intended functionality; in other
words – it may not exist, but its main function is still being performed."
No Altshuller citation on that page. Ellen Domb, "Ideality and Idealized
Design", TRIZ Journal, 2007-02-23,
https://the-trizjournal.com/ideality-and-idealized-design/ [secondary]:
"the ideal final result is the benefits with no costs and no harm" and
"the problem solves itself–the benefits are delivered with no complicating
system." Formula Ideality = Σ benefits / (Σ costs + Σ harm).

Anatomy [secondary]: Wikipedia, "Laws of technical systems evolution",
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laws_of_technical_systems_evolution, citing
Altshuller 1975/1979: "Law of the completeness of the parts of the system.
Any working system must have 4 parts: the engine, the transmission, the
working unit (working organ) and the control element (organ of steering)."
And "Law of increasing the degree of ideality ... The ideal final result
would have all the benefits at zero cost."

Gap finding: state the IFR for the problem ("the system itself performs the
function"), then measure the present design by what it still costs and
harms, and by which of the four parts is weakest or missing. The
"itself" formulation and the four-part completeness law were not read in
Altshuller's own text this session [not verified at source].

## 3. Peter Checkland — Soft Systems Methodology

Works: *Systems Thinking, Systems Practice*, Wiley 1981; with Scholes, *Soft
Systems Methodology in Action*, Wiley 1990; with Poulter, *Learning for
Action*, Wiley 2006. Primary texts not reached; read through Jesper Simonsen,
"Soft Systems Methodology: An Introduction", Roskilde 2000,
http://www.jespersimonsen.dk/Downloads/SSM-IntroductionJS.pdf, which cites
page numbers in the 1981 and 1990 books [secondary]; Bob Williams, "Soft
Systems Methodology", https://www.bobwilliams.co.nz/ewExternalFiles/ssm.pdf
[secondary]; Wikipedia [secondary].

Idea (Checkland 1981 p. 228, via Simonsen) [secondary]: real-world activity is
always "one particular how related to a what which is usually implicit rather
than explicit." The conceptual model holds the whats; the comparison is "a
confrontation of 'whats' with 'hows'."

Anatomy. Seven stages (1981): 1 problem situation; 2 situation expressed (rich
picture); 3 root definitions of relevant systems; 4 conceptual models;
5 "Comparison of 4 with 2"; 6 feasible and desirable changes; 7 action.
CATWOE (1981 pp. 224ff, via Simonsen) [secondary]: Customers, Actors,
Transformation process, Weltanschauung, Owners, Environmental constraints.
PQR (1990 p. 39): "do X by Y in order to achieve Z." The three Es (1990
p. 39): efficacy "does the means work?", efficiency "amount of output divided
by amount of resources used", effectiveness "is T meeting the longer term
aim?"

Gap finding (Williams, after Checkland) [secondary]: "take each component of a
model and the interrelationships between them, and ask ... Does it exist in
the real world, and if so, how does it exist? How does it behave in the real
world? How is its performance identified and evaluated? Is it any good in
terms of the worldview?" The comparison yields a list of discrepancies, which
becomes the agenda for change. This is the most explicit "what is missing"
procedure found.

## 4. Christopher Alexander — centers, wholeness, fifteen properties; and misfits

Work: *The Nature of Order*, Book One, *The Phenomenon of Life*, Center for
Environmental Structure, 2002. Alexander's own summary essay read at
https://topologicalmedialab.net/xinwei/classes/readings/Alexander/Nature%20of%20Order/Alexander_Nature%20of%20Order.htm
[source]; the property list read in Iba & Sakai, "Understanding Christopher
Alexander's Fifteen Properties via Visualization and Analysis", PURPLSOC 2014,
https://web.sfc.keio.ac.jp/~iba/papers/PURPLSOC14_Properties.pdf, citing
Alexander 2002 [secondary].

Idea (Alexander's essay) [source]: "Wholeness is composed of centers." "I
defined a center as a field-like centrality that occurs in space. It is not an
object. It is not a point." "Degree of life is an objective quality that may be
measured by empirical methods."

Anatomy (Iba & Sakai, citing Alexander 2002) [secondary]: "(1) LEVELS OF SCALE,
(2) STRONG CENTERS, (3) BOUNDARIES, (4) ALTERNATING REPETITION, (5) POSITIVE
SPACE, (6) GOOD SHAPE, (7) LOCAL SYMMETRIES, (8) DEEP INTERLOCK AND AMBIGUITY,
(9) CONTRAST, (10) GRADIENTS, (11) ROUGHNESS, (12) ECHOES, (13) THE VOID, (14)
SIMPLICITY AND INNER CALM, and (15) NOT-SEPARATENESS."

Gap finding [source]: the mirror-of-the-self test, "a test that asks which of
two things induces the greater wholeness in the observer" and "which of the
two most nearly resembles the observer's inner self"; plus checking each
center for which of the fifteen properties it lacks.

Earlier work, *Notes on the Synthesis of Form*, Harvard 1964, read via Richard
P. Gabriel, "Notes on 'Notes on the Synthesis of Form'", *Programming* 2023,
https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.12087 [secondary, quotes Alexander]: "it is the
easiest thing in the world to name the specific kinds of misfit which prevent
good fit." "Because they are expressed in negative form they are specific, and
tangible enough to talk about." Here the ideal (good fit between form and
context) is defined as the absence of an enumerated list of misfits; the list
of misfits is the benchmark.

## 5. Aristotle — the four causes

Work: *Physics* II.3 (194b16–195a3), trans. R. P. Hardie and R. K. Gaye,
https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/physics.2.ii.html [source].

Idea and anatomy [source]: "(1) that out of which a thing comes to be and
which persists, is called 'cause', e.g. the bronze of the statue"; "(2) the
form or the archetype, i.e. the statement of the essence ... (e.g. of the
octave the relation of 2:1)"; "(3) the primary source of the change or coming
to rest; e.g. ... the father is cause of the child"; "(4) in the sense of end
or 'that for the sake of which' a thing is done, e.g. health is the cause of
walking about."

Gap finding: Aristotle gives no comparison procedure; the four causes are the
questions a complete account must answer. A design is checked by asking which
of material, form, maker/agency, and end it leaves unstated.

## 6. Plato — the form as the maker's reference

Work: *Republic* X (596a–597e), trans. Benjamin Jowett,
https://classics.mit.edu/Plato/republic.11.x.html [source].

Idea [source]: three beds, "one existing in nature, which is made by God",
"another which is the work of the carpenter", and the painter's, who "is
thrice removed from the king and from the truth." The craftsman works to the
form: "the maker of either of them makes a bed or he makes a table for our
use, in accordance with the idea."

Anatomy: none enumerated; the form is single and the standard. Gap finding is
by distance from the form (first, second, third remove). This is the oldest
statement of the ideal as the reference the made thing is measured against.

## 7. Vitruvius — firmitas, utilitas, venustas

Work: *De Architectura* I.3.2 (c. 15 BC). Gwilt translation at
https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Vitruvius/1*.html [source];
Morgan translation at https://humanitiesinaminute.ie.edu/?p=3324 [source].

Idea and anatomy (Morgan) [source]: "All these must be built with due
reference to durability, convenience, and beauty." "Durability will be assured
when foundations are carried down to the solid ground and materials wisely
and liberally selected; convenience, when the arrangement of the apartments
is faultless and presents no hindrance to use ... and beauty, when the
appearance of the work is pleasing and in good taste, and when its members
are in due proportion."

Also I.2.1 (Gwilt) [source]: architecture "depends on fitness (ordinatio) and
arrangement (dispositio) ... proportion, uniformity, consistency, and economy"
— six principles beside the three qualities.

Gap finding: implicit; a building lacking any of the three is deficient. The
triad is the shortest complete anatomy found.

## 8. Herbert A. Simon — the artifact as interface; inner and outer environment

Work: *The Sciences of the Artificial*, MIT Press, 1969; 3rd ed. 1996, ch. 1
and ch. 5 [source, 3rd ed. PDF].

Idea [source]: "An artifact can be thought of as a meeting point an
'interface' in today's terms between an 'inner' environment, the substance and
organization of the artifact itself, and an 'outer' environment, the
surroundings in which it operates." "Fulfillment of purpose or adaptation to a
goal involves a relation among three terms: the purpose or goal, the
character of the artifact, and the environment in which the artifact
performs."

Anatomy [source]: the three terms above (goal, artifact/inner environment,
outer environment); and "four indicia that distinguish the artificial from the
natural": "1. Artificial things are synthesized ... by human beings. 2. ...
may imitate appearances in natural things while lacking ... the reality of
the latter. 3. ... can be characterized in terms of functions, goals,
adaptation. 4. ... are often discussed ... in terms of imperatives as well as
descriptives." Design (ch. 5): "Everyone designs who devises courses of action
aimed at changing existing situations into preferred ones."

Gap finding: "If the inner environment is appropriate to the outer
environment, or vice versa, the artifact will serve its intended purpose."
A design is checked for whether each of the three terms is stated and
whether the interface fits.

## 9. Stafford Beer — the Viable System Model

Works: *Brain of the Firm* 1972; *The Heart of Enterprise* 1979; *Diagnosing
the System for Organizations* 1985; "The Viable System Model: its
provenance, development, methodology and pathology", *J. Opl Res. Soc.* 35
(1984), reprinted Cwarel Isaf Institute,
https://www.kybernetik.ch/dwn/Viable_System_Model.pdf [source].

Idea [source]: "there are five necessary and sufficient subsystems
interactively involved in any organism or organization that is capable of
maintaining its identity independently of other such organisms within a
shared environment." "The 'fiveness' was due to my efforts to establish the
necessary and sufficient conditions of viability, and five was their number."
Recursion: "any viable system contains, and is contained in, a viable system."

Anatomy [source, Beer's annotations; wording of each role from the ISSS
primer https://web3.isss.org/primer2/beer.html, secondary]: System 1 the
operations that produce the system ("its autopoietic generators"); System 2
coordination, damping oscillation; System 3 internal control and resource
bargain, with 3* audit; System 4 outside-and-future; System 5 policy,
balancing 3 and 4, "embody supreme rules and norms."

Gap finding [source]: "Such breakdowns may be diagnosed, simply in the fact
that some inadequacy in the system can be traced to malfunction in one of the
five subsystems." "If Two is missing, activity in One can turn deadly and
self-defeating ... if Four is missing, Three and Five can collapse into each
other." Diagnosis asks whether each of the five exists and whether its
channels carry requisite variety.

## 10. Reference models and golden models

OASIS, *Reference Model for Service Oriented Architecture 1.0*, OASIS
Standard, 2006-10-12, https://docs.oasis-open.org/soa-rm/v1.0/soa-rm.html
[source]: "A reference model is an abstract framework for understanding
significant relationships among the entities of some environment." "A
reference model is not directly tied to any standards, technologies or other
concrete implementation details." Layering: reference model → reference
architectures and patterns → "Concrete architectures arise from a combination
of reference architectures, architectural patterns" (section 1.2, Figure 1).

ISO/IEC 7498-1 (OSI Basic Reference Model, 1984/1994), quoted at
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OSI_model [secondary]: the model "provides a
common basis for the coordination of standards development for the purpose
of systems interconnection." Anatomy: seven layers, Physical, Data Link,
Network, Transport, Session, Presentation, Application.

Hardware golden model, ChipVerify, "Pass/fail criteria and scoreboards",
https://chipverify.com/verification/pass-fail-criteria-scoreboards
[source, practitioner page]: "The reference model is the ground truth that
the scoreboard compares against." Expected values come from "either
pre-computed golden references or the outputs of a reference model running in
parallel"; the scoreboard "dequeues the corresponding expected value, and
compares them."

Gap finding: a design is checked against the reference model by mapping each
of its entities to a model entity (SOA-RM, OSI); a hardware design is checked
transaction by transaction against the golden model's output. The reference
model names what must exist; the golden model names what each output must be.

## 11. Nam P. Suh — axiomatic design

Works: *The Principles of Design*, Oxford 1990; *Axiomatic Design*, Oxford
2001. Not reached; read via Lo Valvo & Licari, ICAD 2004-43,
https://www.axiomaticdesign.com/wp-content/uploads/icad-2004-43.pdf
[secondary].

Idea and anatomy [secondary]: four domains with vectors — customer attributes
{CA}, functional requirements {FR}, design parameters {DP}, process variables
{PV}; "Axiom 1: The independence Axiom. Maintain the independence of
functional requirement. Axiom 2: The information Axiom. Minimize the
information contents of the design." "An ideal design matrix is a square
diagonal"; "In ideal design each functional requirement must be linked to one
design parameter."

Gap finding: write the FR→DP matrix; a missing DP (fewer DPs than FRs) means
an FR cannot be satisfied; off-diagonal terms mean coupling. The ideal
(square, diagonal) is the benchmark.

## 12. Max Weber — the ideal type

Work: "'Objectivity' in Social Science and Social Policy", 1904; trans. Shils
and Finch, *The Methodology of the Social Sciences*, Free Press 1949, p. 90.
Read via https://people.duke.edu/~jmoody77/TheoryNotes/weber_OSS_BasicTerms.htm
and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideal_type [secondary].

Idea [secondary]: "An ideal type is formed by the one-sided accentuation of
one or more points of view ... In its conceptual purity, this mental construct
cannot be found empirically anywhere in reality. It is a utopia." The task of
research is then "determining in each individual case, the extent to which
this ideal-construct approximates to or diverges from reality" [not verified
at source; wording from search summaries only].

Anatomy: none fixed; each ideal type enumerates its own accentuated traits.
Gap finding: by approximation or divergence of the case from the type.

## Observations

Two families appear. (a) The ideal as a *limit* with no parts: Plato's form,
Altshuller's IFR, Weber's utopia, Ackoff's "best ideal-seeking system its
designers can conceive"; here the gap is a distance. (b) The ideal as an
*enumerated anatomy*: Aristotle's four causes, Vitruvius's three qualities,
Altshuller's four parts, Simon's three terms, Beer's five subsystems,
Alexander's fifteen properties, Checkland's CATWOE and three Es, Suh's FR/DP
matrix, OSI's seven layers; here the gap is a missing or weak part.

The explicit "walk the model and ask of each part: does it exist, how, how
well" procedure is Checkland's stage 5 (via Williams) and Beer's pathology
("if Two is missing ..."). Ackoff is the only one found who orders the whole
process ideal-first and then names "the gaps between this idealized design
and the organization as it is" as the output of a stage. Alexander (1964)
is the only one who defines the ideal negatively, as the absence of a listed
set of misfits.

No source was found that combines these into a single named method of
"ideal-system anatomy as a what-are-we-missing benchmark"; the synthesis
above is this flow's, not any author's.
